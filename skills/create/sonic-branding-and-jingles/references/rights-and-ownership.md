# Rights and ownership for a sonic identity (checked 2026-10)

Read this before step 5. It is orientation, not legal advice. A sonic logo is meant to be used for years, so a
trademark or music lawyer's hour is cheap compared with replacing it later.

## Three separate questions

| Question | What decides it | Where to look |
|---|---|---|
| May I use this audio commercially? | the generating tool's terms and your plan tier | the tool's terms page, saved with the date |
| Do I own it, and can I stop others copying it? | copyright: human authorship | your motif, arrangement and edits |
| Can it identify my brand like a logo? | trademark: a registered sound mark, or use-based rights | a trademark office and a lawyer |

## 1. Tool terms

From the licensing matrix in [../../music-and-sound-for-shorts/references/licensing-matrix.md](../../music-and-sound-for-shorts/references/licensing-matrix.md):

- **ElevenLabs Music** (`elevenlabs/music-v1`, `elevenlabs/music-v2.5`): marketed as cleared for commercial use; the
  Music Terms bar six sectors (weapons, tobacco, prescription drugs, adult content, religious organisations, political
  advocacy); prompts may not name artists, songs, labels or publishers or include substantial lyrics; **outputs may
  not be unique to you**.
- **ElevenLabs SFX** (`elevenlabs/sfx-v2`): covered by ElevenLabs' general terms for your plan; keep the plan tier on
  record.
- **Google Lyria** (`google/lyria-3.5`, `google/lyria-3-clip`): SynthID watermark on every output; no clear published
  commercial or monetisation terms beyond Google's general terms. Don't build a brand signature on it until someone has
  read the current terms and recorded the decision.

"May not be unique" matters more for a sonic logo than for a background bed: the same or a very similar sound could be
generated for someone else. The fix is a human-written motif (below).

## 2. Copyright: human authorship

- The US Copyright Office's report *Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2: Copyrightability* (2025-01-29)
  reaffirms that copyright protects human authorship. Purely AI-generated material isn't protected; prompts alone don't
  give enough control; human-authored material that is perceptible in the output, and creative selection, arrangement
  or modification of AI material, can be protected. (Summaries by Mintz, Baker Botts and others; read the report on
  copyright.gov for the exact wording.)
- Other countries differ; the human-authorship principle is common but not universal.
- What to keep as evidence: the dated DAW project with your MIDI motif, the takes you played, the edit decisions, and
  the media sidecars for each AI layer (prompt, model, date).

Practical rule: the **motif** (the notes and rhythm people remember) should be written and played by a person. AI can
supply textures, alternates and arrangements around it.

## 3. Trademark: sound marks

- In the US, sounds can be registered when they are "arbitrary, unique or distinctive" and work as a source indicator.
  Commonplace sounds, or sounds goods make in normal operation (alarm clocks, phones), are not inherently distinctive
  and need proof of acquired distinctiveness under §2(f) (TMEP §1202.15).
- Applications include an audio specimen (formats such as .wav, .mp3; TEAS limits the file size, around 5 MB per the
  TMEP specimen section §904.03(f)) and a written description of the sound.
- Other offices (EUIPO, UKIPO and others) accept sound marks under their own rules. File only after the sound is final
  and in use; a lawyer can search for conflicts first.

## Soundalike check

1. List well-known sonic logos and stings in your category and in platforms your audience uses.
2. Play your candidate next to each. Same first three notes and rhythm? Change it.
3. Ask five people "does this remind you of anything?" without telling them what you're worried about.
4. Log the check (date, who, what you compared) in the asset kit.

## Using third-party sounds inside the kit

- Library SFX (a camera click, a vinyl crackle) are fine as texture if the library licence allows use in a brand asset;
  many royalty-free licences forbid using the sound **as** a logo or trademark. Read that clause.
- Never sample a song, a game sound or another brand's sound.
